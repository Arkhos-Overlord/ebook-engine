# Chapter 5: Marketing That Actually Ships

Every small business owner I have met has a marketing plan. Very few have a marketing history — a record of work that consistently went out. The gap between the two is not ideas or ambition. It is Tuesdays. It is the specific, accumulated friction of sitting down at 3pm with forty minutes of margin left in the day and needing to invent a post from nothing.

AI does not fix your strategy. It does not know who your customers are, what your best channel is, or whether you should be on video. Those are still your decisions, and Chapter 1's warning applies: be suspicious of anything claiming otherwise. What AI fixes is the invention-from-nothing problem, which is the actual reason most content never ships. Consistently shipping mediocre-in-strategy content with real business substance beats a brilliant post per quarter, every time, because the first is a machine that compounds and the second is a diary entry.

This chapter is about building that machine around content you already have.

## Start With What Exists

You do not need a content well. You need a look at the things you already produce without noticing.

Open the last month and find them: the explanations you gave customers on the phone, which are posts; the questions people asked before buying, which are headlines; the job you finished that you photographed anyway, which is a case study; the mistake you see competitors making, which is a point of view; the before-and-after in your camera roll, which is proof. A window fitter I worked with had two years of photos of condensation problems and never once wrote the caption that explains why they happen — because writing captions felt like marketing, and marketing felt like a separate activity he was bad at.

It is not a separate activity. It is a translation job: take what you know and already demonstrated this week, and change the format so someone scrolling can use it. Translation is exactly what these tools do well, provided you supply the source material — which you have, in your sent messages, your job photos, your customer questions.

So the first move is a harvest, not a brainstorm. Spend twenty minutes collecting ten raw items: three customer questions, three photos or job outcomes, two things you explained recently, one opinion you hold about your industry, one number from your business you are comfortable sharing. That is a month of content, and the next section turns it into one.

## The Assembly Line

Here is the workflow, and the important part is that it produces variants, because the same substance belongs on more than one surface.

Take one raw item — say, the customer question "how often should I really service the boiler?" Paste it with your two-line credentials (what you do, where) and ask for three things at once: a short social post answering it in your voice, a two-paragraph email version for your list, and a one-line version suitable as a reply in comments or DMs.

One item in, three formats out, four minutes total including your edit. Then move to the next item. Batch it: once a week, sit for forty-five minutes, run all ten items through, and you have a month of multi-format content drafted while you still remember why each one mattered.

Two quality guards. First, the numbers and claims get your Chapter 3 fact-check pass — models will confidently produce statistics about your industry that do not exist, and "studies show" is a phrase to delete unless you know the study. Second, the specifics are what make it yours: the actual suburb you covered, the real model of machine, the honest price range. Generic content is not "on brand," it is invisible, and the model will drift generic on you the moment you let it. Anchor every draft to one concrete detail from the raw item.

## The Calendar Question

Frequent enough to address directly: how often should you post? The honest answer is the frequency you can sustain without the content getting thin, which for most small businesses is two to four times a week on one channel, not daily everywhere. Choose the channel where your customers already are — usually the one you use yourself — and be quietly excellent there before opening a second front.

The batch workflow makes three or four a week almost free, and it has a side effect worth noting: when drafting takes minutes instead of an afternoon, the pressure comes off, and the content gets better. People write worse when they are resenting the task. Give someone forty minutes and a tool that does the typing, and what comes out is calmer, more specific, and more like them — the opposite of what most people expect from AI-assisted writing, and consistently what happens.

Scheduling matters here too, and almost every platform has a free built-in scheduler — use it, during the same batch session. The failure mode is not writing the posts; it is writing them and leaving them in your drafts to die. Draft, edit, schedule, in one sitting, and the month is genuinely done.

## The Email List

If you sell anything with a repeat purchase or a considered decision, your email list is the most valuable asset in this chapter, and it is probably being neglected for the same reason as everything else: each send used to require inventing a newsletter from scratch.

Reframe it. An email to your list is not a newsletter. It is the message you would send a customer who asked you a question last Tuesday — helpful, specific, signed by you — sent to everyone who has asked to hear from you. One of those a fortnight, built from the same raw items as your social content, is more than enough.

The workflow: paste the raw item, ask for a short email with a specific subject line and no marketing language, edit for your voice, add one sentence only you could write, send. Keep a swipe file of your three best-performing subject lines — your own, not generic examples — and feed them in as the example when you ask for subject line options. Ask for five options, pick one; do not let the model pick for you, because your customers' taste is not in the model, it is in your sent folder.

## The Website That Stops Waiting

There is one piece of marketing content every small business needs that genuinely stalls for months, because it is a blank page with a domain name attached: the About page, the service descriptions, the FAQ. Blank pages feel permanent in a way that social posts do not. Nobody wants to write the wrong version of the sentence that defines their business.

AI is the best cure for blank-page paralysis I have found, for a specific reason: a mediocre first draft is easy to improve and impossible to start. So start badly, deliberately. Ask for a rough draft of the About page given your facts and a note that you will rewrite it. It will be generic — fine, that is the clay. Then spend twenty minutes cutting everything that is not true of you specifically, adding the two details a customer would care about (how fast, how much, who does the work), and reading it aloud until it sounds like you.

The FAQ writes itself especially well, because it is literally a list of questions you have answered before: paste ten real ones from your inbox, ask for concise answers with your facts supplied, edit, publish. A good FAQ is also the gift that keeps giving — it deflates the repeat enquiries that were eating your Chapter 4 hours, which is the only true cure for that particular leak. You end up automating your own inbox by making the customers smarter, not the replies faster.

## What Not to Bother With

The trap in this chapter is the tool-shaped version of the strategy problem: an AI that will generate five hundred posts in an afternoon, spin up ten niche sites, or run twelve accounts on autopilot. Resist it. Readers detect content that was made without a person in the loop, platforms increasingly penalize it, and a customer who spots a templated reply on their specific problem now doubts everything else you said. The moat in a small business is being recognizable as a person — cheap content erodes exactly the asset you cannot buy back.

Related: do not chase every new format because a video told you the algorithm rewards it. The algorithm rewards consistency, which is unglamorous, and consistency is what you just built.

## This Week

Do the twenty-minute harvest — ten raw items, written down. Then run two of them through the assembly line: three formats each, fact-checked, edited, and — this is the part that counts — one of them published or sent before the week ends. Open your email platform and send a real one to your list if you have one; if you do not, this week you create it, with a one-line signup promise you can keep. Draft and schedule next week's batch in the same sitting. The goal is not a hit post. The goal is to end the week with a system that no longer depends on you having a good Tuesday.
