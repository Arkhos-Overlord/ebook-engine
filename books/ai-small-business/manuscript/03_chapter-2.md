# Chapter 2: The Ten-Hour Audit

The reason most AI experiments fail has nothing to do with the technology. They fail because the owner asks the tool to "help with my business" the way you would ask a new hire to "make yourself useful," and gets back something generic because they handed over a generic problem. Garbage in, cliché out.

The audit fixes this by making you specific before you make the tool work. It takes about ninety minutes spread over a week, and it is the single highest-leverage thing in this book. Every hour you spend here pays back in the next six chapters, because when you reach the prompt for writing customer replies, you will know exactly how many of those replies you send, in which situations, and what they have in common.

## The Log (Three Days)

You started this in the introduction; if you skipped it, start now. For three normal workdays, note every task switch with a rough duration. The discipline that matters is honesty about the small stuff: the four minutes re-reading an email to remember the context, the nine minutes finding where the supplier's invoice went, the two-minute check of yesterday's messages that you will do again three more times today.

At the end of three days you will have a list that looks absurd — sixty to a hundred line items for most owners — and that absurdity is the point. The feeling of being busy is not made of big things. It is made of forty small ones.

Do not do this on your best day or your worst. Do not change your behavior for the log. The temptation is to perform a tidier version of your week for the notebook; resist it, because you are measuring the actual one.

## The Sort

Take the list and give every item one of four labels. Use a highlighter, a spreadsheet, or the back of an envelope — the tool does not matter.

**YOU.** Work only you can do, because of judgment, relationship, or authority. The conversation with the customer who has been with you eight years. Pricing a job where one wrong number eats the whole margin. Firing, hiring, deciding. This pile should feel small and valuable.

**YOU, BUT LEANER.** Work that is genuinely yours but bloated — where the first eighty percent could be pre-drafted, templated, or researched in advance, and your judgment is really only needed on the last twenty percent. Writing the proposal. The monthly report to a partner. The job description. This pile is where AI quietly transforms your hours without changing a single responsibility.

**MACHINE-READY.** Patterned work with a repeatable shape: the same six kinds of email, the scheduling, the data transfer between systems, the social captions, the first-pass research, the invoice chasing, the meeting notes. This pile is the ten hours.

**DELEGATE OR DELETE.** Work that should not happen at all, or should happen less — the fourth weekly check of the same inbox, the meeting that is a status update, the custom nobody ordered. AI cannot fix this pile, and pretending otherwise is how people end up automating work that should have been cancelled. Cross it out and enjoy the rare luxury of a productivity book telling you to do less.

Now add up the MACHINE-READY column. Almost everyone who does this rigorously lands between eight and fifteen hours a week, which is where the title of this book comes from. If you landed at four, congratulations — you are unusually focused, and the applications chapters will still save you the trouble of the work you hate. If you landed at twenty-five, do not panic: you are not going to automate all of it this quarter, you are going to automate the easiest fifth of it and use the momentum.

## The Ranking

You cannot do everything at once, and trying to is the classic failure — five half-finished experiments, no workflow that actually runs, and the quiet conclusion that "AI is overhyped." So rank the Machine-Ready pile on two axes.

**Volume:** how many times a week does this happen? Multiply count by minutes. The reply-to-price-enquiry item will often beat the once-a-month item on raw hours, even if the once-a-month item feels more impressive.

**Pain:** how much do you personally resent it? This is not soft. A task you avoid gets done late, and lateness has a price — the quote sent three days after the enquiry, the review answered two weeks after it was posted, the invoice chased a month after it was due. Resentment is a signal about where money leaks.

Something usually scores high on both, and it is almost always one of these five: replying to new enquiries, chasing money that is owed, creating the week's marketing content, writing quotes and proposals, or keeping up with messages across three or four channels. Chapters 4 through 8 take those five in order. If your top item is something else — a tradesperson's scheduling, a consultant's research summaries, a retailer's product listings — the method is identical; only the prompt details change, and the appendix will show you how to adapt.

## The Realistic Baseline

One more measurement, and then we are done with the clipboard. For your top-ranked task, find out what it actually costs you today, in time and in money, before any AI touches it.

Concretely: count how many price enquiries came in last month, time how long one reply really takes from opening the message to pressing send (include the re-reading, the checking of the price list, the moments you got distracted), and multiply. Do this for two or three tasks.

This number is your scoreboard. Write it somewhere you will see it — a sticky note on the monitor, a line in your diary. In ninety days you will compare against it, and the comparison needs to be against the honest week you just measured, not against a vague memory of being busy. Owners who skip this step almost always undervalue what they have built, decide it did not work, and go back to typing the replies themselves.

While you are measuring, note the failure cost too. Last month, how many enquiries went unanswered? How many quotes went out late? How many invoices sat an extra week? These are not your fault, exactly — they are symptoms of a week with no slack in it — but they are the hidden line item, and reducing it may be worth more than the hours themselves. A quote that reaches a customer on Tuesday instead of Friday is, in most businesses, a different quote.

## What About My Team?

If you employ people, run the audit with them — and run it as their week, not yours. The patterned work in a small business is frequently sitting in someone else's chair: the junior who types the same delivery updates, the receptionist who fields the same eight questions, the bookkeeper who re-formats the same statements.

This conversation needs care, because "I am measuring your tasks so I can hand them to a machine" lands badly if handled carelessly. Handle it openly instead: the business is getting a new tool, you want to find the repetitive work that eats everyone's day, and the aim is that the tool takes the boring half so people can spend time on the parts that need them. That is also, in practice, the truth — small businesses do not usually fire people over admin savings, they stop needing to hire for the next two years, and the people who stay get to do more interesting work.

The people doing a job also know things about it that no log captures: which customer's messages always need a human reply, where the data entry actually goes wrong, the workaround for the software quirk everyone has silently worked around for three years. Interview them for ten minutes each. Those details are the difference between an automation that works in the demo and one that survives a real Wednesday.

## This Week

Complete the three-day log, do the sort, rank your Machine-Ready pile, and write the baseline numbers for your top two tasks on a sticky note. Ninety minutes total, spread across five days. When you turn to Chapter 3, you will not be learning abstract prompting techniques — you will be learning to get output from a tool for a task you have already measured, named, and priced. That is a very different, and much shorter, learning curve.
