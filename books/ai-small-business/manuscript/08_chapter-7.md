# Chapter 7: The Back Office (Invoices, Scheduling, Paperwork, Reports)

The unglamorous chapter. Nothing here will go viral and none of it will impress anyone at a dinner party, which is precisely why this work is still sitting in your week while the exciting AI content got all the attention. The back office is where patterned work goes to breed, and it is often half the hours in the audit.

The rule for everything in this chapter is the same and worth stating once, because it governs all of it: the model prepares, you approve, the system of record stays the system of record. AI drafts the invoice text; your accounting software remains where money lives. AI summarizes the week; your numbers still come from your actual books. Nothing about this chapter makes the model a bookkeeper, a scheduler, or an authority. It makes those jobs faster to do the way you already do them.

## Chasing Money

Start where the pain is highest. Unpaid invoices are the most common slow leak in a small business: not dramatic enough to trigger a collections process, annoying enough that everyone avoids the task, and expensive in a way that compounds quietly while you pretend not to look.

The workflow has three parts. First, the nudge itself. Write one firm-but-fair payment reminder — you have sent a hundred, pick the tone you would use with a customer you want to keep — and keep it as your template with a placeholder for the details. Second, the escalation ladder: ask the model for three versions at increasing firmness (friendly nudge, formal reminder with late-fee language if your terms have it, final notice before handing to collections), checked against whatever your contract or terms actually say, because the model will happily propose late fees you never agreed to. Third, the list: export your overdue invoices from your accounting software and ask for a priority order — largest, oldest, and any customer with a pattern of lateness float to the top.

Then you do the part that stays human: making the call when a nudge does not move, deciding who gets grace, knowing which customer is mid-crisis and will pay Friday. None of that is automatable and all of it is judgment. What the workflow removes is the reason the list goes untouched — the small dread of composing the message for the tenth time.

Owners who run this weekly rather than monthly usually discover the amounts are smaller and friendlier than the dread implied. The dread was never about the money. It was about the confrontation you were rehearsing, and a drafted message you approve in four seconds is not a confrontation, it is admin.

## Scheduling and Coordination

Two people trying to find an hour is a surprisingly large fraction of a small business's communication, and it generates the same shape of message every time: here is when I can come, does one of these work, confirmed, reminder, running late, rescheduled. All of it draftable in seconds.

The confirmation and reminder messages are where the real hours hide, because their cost is not per-message, it is per forgotten one — every no-show and every "I thought it was Thursday" is unpaid rework. Short, specific reminders that include the actual details (address, what to have ready, parking, duration) are the fix, and they are exactly the kind of routine-but-variable text these tools produce cleanly: give it the appointment details, ask for three sentences with the specifics in, approve, send.

Go one step further if your booking tool supports it and ask whether it has AI features before you buy anything new. The most common expensive mistake in this space is purchasing a dedicated AI scheduling product when the calendar you already pay for added the same capability last update. Your tools have been quietly gaining features — Chapter 10 makes an audit of this explicit — and using what you own is free.

## Documents You Only Write Twice a Year

The category that punishes small businesses most unfairly: the job description, the policy page, the supplier agreement template, the employee handbook section, the standard operating procedure. Rarely written, always dreaded, disproportionately important when they finally get used, and almost always started from a blank page at the worst possible moment — because they are needed the moment someone is hired, or a process breaks, or a customer complains about a policy you never wrote down.

The blank-page problem is the same one from Chapter 5 and it dissolves the same way. You bring the facts and the situation; the model brings the structure and the boilerplate you would have copied from a template site anyway. A job description is a list of duties you already know, in the standard shape candidates expect. A policy is a decision you have already made, stated consistently. An SOP is a process you can already perform, written down in order — and if you want a genuinely good one, dictate the process out loud as if explaining it to a new hire, paste the transcript, and ask for it to be structured into numbered steps with a tools list and a quality check at the end.

That last trick — talking instead of typing — deserves to become a habit generally. Models read messy spoken transcripts remarkably well, and dictating while walking or driving removes the typing barrier that stops most documentation from ever happening. The procedures that exist in your head are a business asset that currently walks out if you get hit by a bus; twenty minutes of dictated, AI-structured SOPs for your core processes converts a liability into a manual. It will not be perfect — the model will occasionally invent a step you do not do, which is why every SOP gets read once by someone who actually does the job before it becomes official.

## The Weekly and Monthly Numbers

Reporting is the task owners most often say they do not need, until the quarter ends and they cannot remember what happened. The monthly admin that eats an evening — what came in, what went out, which jobs were profitable, what is owed, what is overdue, are we ahead or behind last month — is a summarization task with the inputs sitting in software that exports to CSV or spreadsheet format.

The workflow: export or screenshot the numbers, paste them, and ask for a one-page summary written for an owner rather than an accountant — top line first, exceptions highlighted, three specific things that need attention, comparisons to the previous period where the data allows. Ask it not to calculate. Calculations from pasted numbers are where models quietly make arithmetic errors that then anchor your decisions; ask it to describe and organize the figures you gave it, and do the sums in the spreadsheet where they are computed rather than recalled.

A habit worth adopting on top: a five-minute Friday version. Not a report — a note to yourself in the same format, three lines, what happened this week and what is pending. Twelve of those make an end-of-year review that would otherwise take a weekend to reconstruct from memory, and they make the decision-making better in between, because "what did we say we would chase" becomes searchable instead of forgotten.

One firm boundary, repeated from the money chapter because it matters more than it sounds: your accountant, your tax filing, and your formal books are not the place for AI assistance. Reading a tax letter and asking for plain-English help is sensible. Letting a model prepare the numbers you file is how a small business discovers, audited and expensive, that confident and correct were never the same thing. The model explains; the professional and the software decide.

## Email: The Final Boss

One more, because you were going to ask. Inbox zero is not an AI problem — it is a boundary problem, and tools will not fix a process that accepts four hundred emails a week as normal. But the drafts inside it are firmly in scope.

The recurring ones are the win: "received, will get to this by Friday," "yes, that works," "here is the file you asked for, attaching now," "let me check and come back to you tomorrow." Short, low-stakes, repeated weekly — paste the thread, ask for a two-line reply, approve, send. You will find four or five of these patterns cover most of what arrives, and the triage list from Chapter 4 (reply-now, reply-today, reply-later) does the sorting before the drafting starts.

The deeper fix is unsubscribing and delegating the rest, but that is Chapter 9's territory — doing less, with AI simply making the remaining volume tolerable while you work the list down.

## This Week

Pick two items from your Machine-Ready pile in this chapter's territory — likely candidates are invoice chasing and one of those twice-a-year documents. Chase every overdue invoice with a drafted nudge this week (check your terms first, keep it factual, send from you). Then write the one document you have been avoiding for months: dictate it, structure it with the model, read it once for invented steps, file it somewhere findable. Note the hours against the baseline. The back office will never be exciting, but a week where it costs you ninety minutes instead of six hours changes what the rest of the week is able to contain.
