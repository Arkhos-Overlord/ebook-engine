# Chapter 6: Quotes, Proposals, and Follow-Up (Where the Money Is)

Of everything in this book, this chapter pays the most direct rent. The reason is arithmetic every owner already knows but rarely measures: in most small businesses, the gap between "enough work" and "too much work" is not lead generation. It is the queue of quotes that never got written, and the follow-ups that never got sent.

The pattern is painfully consistent. Enquiry arrives, you are on a job, you will "get to it tonight." Tonight becomes Thursday. The customer, who was ready to buy on Tuesday, has heard from two competitors since. You send yours anyway; it is fine, it is late, and late quotes win less often. Then the ones that do not win never get chased, because chasing feels like begging and the week is full.

Both halves of that are draftable work. Let's take them in order.

## The Quote

A good quote is a document with a structure, and structures are the model's home ground. Yours probably looks like: what you understand they want, what you will do, what it costs, what is not included, when you can start, how to say yes. Write that skeleton once — five lines in a document — and every future quote starts from it.

The workflow when an enquiry comes in. First, capture the facts: paste their message or your site-visit notes, your standard scope language, your rates or the numbers you have calculated, the date you can start, your payment terms. Then ask for a draft with the structure filled in, in your voice, with line items where you normally have line items.

Two things the model gets genuinely right here, worth noting because skeptics expect it to fail at both. It keeps scope language consistent between quotes, which stops the slow drift where every quote is worded slightly differently and a customer later points at the wording. And it is unhurried about the details — the deposit line, the "valid for 30 days" line, the exclusions — which are exactly the things tired humans skip and regret later. You will still check every number, every date, and every scope statement against what you actually agreed on the visit. Numbers are where models slip, and a quote is a promise. But the assembling, the phrasing, the consistency: seconds instead of forty minutes.

The quality difference is worth pausing on, because it surprises people. Not "fast and worse" — often better. When you are drafting a quote at 9pm for the fourth time that day, the scope exclusions get terse and the tone gets curt. When the draft arrives in five seconds and you are only editing, you are in a better state of mind, and it shows. Customers can feel the difference between a quote cobbled together in exhaustion and one that reads like it came from a company in control. The second one also justifies the price in it more comfortably — tone genuinely moves what people will pay.

## The Follow-Up Sequence

This is the single highest-return automation in the book, and I want to be precise about why.

A quote that is accepted on first contact is the exception. The norm is silence: not rejection, not disinterest — just a customer who got distracted, or is waiting for the other quote, or meant to reply and did not. The industry shorthand is that most sales happen on the follow-up, and most small businesses send zero or one, because each follow-up is a small act of emotional risk that you keep postponing.

Automating the drafting removes the emotional risk along with the friction. You are not asking the model to be persuasive; you are asking it to be polite, prompt, and consistent — three things it does not struggle with.

Design it as a sequence, three touches, starting the day after the quote goes out. The first, a day later: brief, warm, assumes nothing is wrong, offers to walk through it — its whole job is to be the nudge the customer needed anyway. The second, four or five days after that: adds something rather than repeating — an answer to a likely question, a note about availability filling up, a relevant detail from the job. The third, two weeks after: closes the loop honestly — we are still happy to do this, no pressure, is the project still live?

Each one is two or three sentences. Feed the model the quote you sent and ask for all three drafts at once; keep the good ones in your message library; send them from you, in your name, because sequences that arrive obviously automated get deleted faster than ones that arrive looking like a person remembered you.

Two disciplines keep this from going wrong. Pause the sequence the moment they reply — a follow-up landing on an active conversation reads as a robot that is not reading, and costs more goodwill than the sequence earns. And cap it at three touches; persistence past that is the behavior people screenshot to complain about. Three reminders over two weeks, then a genuine stop, and you have covered every reasonable customer without ever being the business that nags.

Track what this is worth for real. Count quotes sent last quarter, estimate your normal win rate honestly, and check what happens to it over the next sixty days. Even a modest lift — one extra job a month converted purely by timely, consistent follow-up — usually dwarfs every other saving in this book combined, because it is revenue rather than cost avoided.

## Proposals That Are Bigger Than Quotes

Some businesses sell a document heavier than a quote: a scope of work, a service agreement, a design proposal with phases. Same workflow, larger payload. What changes is the input — you give the model the client's own words back (their brief, their emails), your standard terms, and a request that the proposal address their stated priorities by name, in order.

That last instruction is where AI earns its keep in longer documents. A proposal that visibly answers the three things the client said they cared about — and repeats their phrasing while doing it — consistently beats a more polished generic one. Reading their brief closely is a discipline that requires attention; the model, given the brief, will not get distracted halfway through it. Your job is the judgment: which commitments to make, what timeline is real, which terms you are not signing.

Never let a model decide those. Terms, liabilities, cancellation clauses, and anything your lawyer or accountant would want to see belong to a human — yours, and if the deal is large enough, a professional's. What you are using AI for is the assembly and the client-specific mapping, not the risk allocation. Keep that line firm and this chapter's time savings are free; cross it and the "savings" become an unpriced liability sitting in a document you signed.

## Pricing Confidence

One unexpected benefit, before we leave: owners who hate quoting often hate it because pricing requires a stance, and the draft gives you somewhere to stand from.

When you ask for a quote with your real numbers in it, you get to see the document as the customer will — the total, what is above it, how the scope reads — while it is still cheap to change. Asking the model for the same quote at a higher price point, as a what-if, costs nothing and takes seconds: "same scope, but price it as if we were the premium option we claim to be; what would you emphasize in the wording?" You do not have to send that version. But owners who run that comparison a few times steadily stop discounting reflexively, because the draft makes the value language concrete and they can see the cheaper number did not say anything more persuasive — it just had a smaller figure attached.

This is a small psychological trick, not an economics lesson. But pricing is half psychology for a small business, and anything that gets you to send the quote with the right number on it, on time, is worth more than any copywriting technique in this chapter.

## This Week

Find one live quote sitting unsent and get it out today using the workflow — facts, structure, numbers checked, send. Then build the follow-up sequence for it: draft all three touches at once, save them as a template in your message library, and put the three send dates in your calendar or set them up in your email tool's scheduled send. Finally, go back through the last month and count the quotes you never sent and the ones you never chased — that number, multiplied by your win rate and your average job, is the figure this chapter is trying to hand you. Write it on the sticky note next to the audit baseline. You are going to want that comparison in ninety days.
