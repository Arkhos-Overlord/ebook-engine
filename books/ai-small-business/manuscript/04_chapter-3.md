# Chapter 3: How to Ask (The Skill That Makes Everything Else Work)

The difference between an owner who gets "AI is a waste of time" and one who gets ten hours back is almost never the tool. Both are using the same model on the same day. The difference is the ask.

I need to deflate something first: you will see people claim there is a dark art to prompting, that certain "power prompts" unlock hidden capability, that models respond to elaborate incantations. Mostly nonsense. Models respond to clarity the way a good employee does. The skill is the boring skill of briefing well — the same one that makes someone good at delegating in general.

Here is everything that matters.

## Give It a Role, Briefly

Opening with who the model should act as narrows the style and the vocabulary it reaches for. It does not give the model knowledge it lacks — a model told "you are a plumber" does not gain plumbing facts it never had — but it does change which parts of what it already knows come forward, and that is worth two sentences.

"You are an experienced small-business accountant writing for a client who is not a finance professional" produces different, better output than "write me something about expenses," even though both draw on the same underlying model. The first tells it the register, the reader, and the standard. The second tells it nothing and gets a brochure back.

Keep it to two or three lines. Long persona essays do not help; they just push your actual request further down the message.

## Give It the Facts It Cannot Have

This is the big one, and it is where most weak output comes from.

The model does not know your business. It does not know your prices, your service area, your tone, your best customer, last week's complaint, or that you never discount in January. If the task depends on any of those facts, you must supply them, or the model will do the only thing it can: fill the gaps with plausible fiction.

Compare these two asks for a reply to an enquiry.

"Write a reply to a customer asking about our prices." — The model has nothing. It will produce something generic about "competitive pricing" and "free consultation," which sounds like every business that has ever been accused of being soulless.

"Write a reply to a customer who asked what we charge for a garden design. We are a two-person studio in Bristol, projects typically start at £1,800, we are booked about six weeks out, and we answer in a friendly but direct tone. They have sent a photo of a small back garden. Keep it under 150 words, do not oversell, and end with a specific next step." — The model now has the reader, the offer, the constraint, the voice, and the shape. The output needs a tweak, not a rewrite.

The pattern: facts, constraints, tone, length, and what "done" looks like. Every missing one is a guess the model makes on your behalf.

A practical habit follows from this: keep a short business fact sheet in a note — what you do, where, typical prices or price ranges, tone of voice with an example of a message you liked, things you never say, common objections, your actual next step for enquiries. Paste the relevant lines into any prompt that touches a customer. Two minutes of setup, and the "generic AI voice" problem mostly disappears.

## Show One Example

When you can, include one good example of the output you want. This is the most reliable quality lever there is, and it is almost never used.

Instead of describing your ideal quote email, paste last month's quote email — the one you were proud of — and say: "Here is the style I use. Write a new one for this job, same voice and structure." The model will mirror your sentence lengths, your headings, your way of handling the deposit line. Describing a style in words is lossy; showing it is not.

Do this a few times per recurring task and something pleasant happens: your templates stop being documents you maintain and become a small library of examples you feed the model each time. The "prompt" for your best task becomes twelve lines of instruction plus the last time you did it well.

## Say What Format You Want

"Give me a summary" is a shrug. "Give me five bullet points, each under twenty words, most important first, ending with the one thing I must not forget" is a spec.

If it is an email: how long, what structure, does it open with acknowledgment or straight to the point, what is the call to action. If it is a document: headings, sections, tone. If it is analysis: the exact question you want answered at the top.

Models follow format instructions closely — far more closely than they follow vague quality words like "professional" or "engaging." Those words mean a different thing to every reader, including the model. Concrete format words — length, structure, ordering, audience — mean the same thing every time.

## Iterate, Do Not Restart

The most common workflow mistake: reading the first reply, wincing, closing the tab, and concluding it did not work. The first reply is a first draft by definition. It is supposed to be approximate.

Instead, talk to it. "Too formal — write it like a text message from a competent friend." "Point 2 is wrong for us, we don't do weekend calls; remove it." "Shorter. Half this length." "Now give me a warmer version of just the opening line."

Two or three turns like that routinely transform a C-grade paragraph into something you would actually send. This is not a failure of the tool; it is the collaboration model. You would do the same with a human drafter. The ten-second pushback loop is the real feature — harder to get with a junior who goes quiet when you critique their work, easier than with anyone you would have to schedule.

A useful trick when the output is going in circles: start a fresh conversation and paste the final version you liked as the example, with the new instruction. Sometimes a conversation accumulates too much of its own earlier wording and keeps drifting back to it. Fresh chat, one good example, one clear instruction — usually fixes a stuck draft.

## Know When to Walk Away

Not every task deserves AI. If the task takes thirty seconds anyway, using the tool is slower than doing it. If the task is deeply personal — the condolence message, the reply that repairs a five-year relationship — write it yourself, badly if necessary, because the authenticity is the point.

More usefully: if you have asked three times and the output is still fighting you, the task is probably under-specified. Stop rephrasing the request and ask the model to interrogate you instead: "Before you draft this, ask me up to five questions that would let you do it well." This single move rescues more dead prompts than any amount of technique. The model is often better at spotting what is missing from your brief than at guessing.

## The Review Pass

Whatever comes back, run it through the same three-question check every time until it is habit. It takes about fifteen seconds and it is the difference between AI making you look sharp and AI getting you into trouble.

**Is anything in here a fact?** Names, numbers, dates, prices, features, regulations. If the draft contains a claim you did not supply, check it or cut it. This catches the hallucinations before a customer does.

**Does this sound like us?** Read the opening line aloud in your head. If it sounds like a pamphlet, rewrite the first sentence — that is usually where the corporate throat-clearing lives — and the rest often follows.

**Would I be comfortable if this exact message got screenshotted?** Because in practice, occasionally it will be. If the answer is no, it is not finished.

Fifteen seconds, three questions, every time. The people who get burned are almost always the people who were in a hurry once.

## This Week

Take your top Machine-Ready task from the audit and do it with AI three times this week — real situations, not practice scenarios. Use the structure: brief with role, supply the facts, specify the format, review with the three questions, iterate instead of restarting. Then compare one of the results to how you would have done it by hand: not just the time, but the quality. Most people are quietly surprised that the AI-assisted version is better, because having to specify the facts forces a clarity that autopilot typing never did.
