# Chapter 10: The Ninety-Day Plan

You now have every piece. What you do not have, almost certainly, is all of them running at once — and doing them one at a time in book order, drifting, is how people reach day sixty with four half-setups and conclude the whole thing was overhyped.

So: a sequence. Twelve weeks, one focused action per week, sized to fit around a real business rather than replace it. The order is deliberate — audit before tooling, prompting before applications before automations — because each week's output is the input to the next. Skip ahead and you will find yourself re-deriving the missing piece later, in worse conditions.

## Weeks 1–2: See the Week You Actually Have

**Week 1 — the audit.** Three-day task log, the four-way sort, the ranked Machine-Ready pile. Write your baseline numbers for the top three tasks on the sticky note: time per week, volume, and any failure costs you can count (missed enquiries, late quotes, unpaid invoices aging). At the end of this week you know what you are chasing and what "done" is worth, which puts you ahead of most people who start by downloading an app.

**Week 2 — the fact sheet and the message library.** The boring setup that everything else leans on. Fifteen lines about your business: what you do, where, prices or ranges, tone with one real example of a message you liked, things you never say, the standard next step for enquiries. Then collect five real messages you have sent that worked — an enquiry reply, a follow-up, a complaint response, a review answer, a quote cover note. One file, plain text, findable. This week also produces your exclusion list from Chapter 9, because you need it before you paste anything anywhere, not after.

Two weeks in, you have no visible productivity gains and a completely different set of instruments. This is the week most people feel tempted to skip ahead. Do not; the next four weeks run at double speed because of it.

## Weeks 3–4: Learn the Ask

**Week 3 — prompting in anger.** Take your top-ranked task and run five real instances through the Chapter 3 structure: role, facts, format, three-question review, iterate rather than restart. Compare each result to your sticky-note baseline — time and, honestly, quality. The conversation you have with yourself in week three, where you discover the drafts are usually good enough and occasionally better than your autopilot version, is the belief change the rest of the plan runs on.

**Week 4 — the second and third tasks.** Same drill, next two items down the ranking. By now the structure is muscle memory and the ramp that took a week for task one takes two days for task three. Note which tasks resist: a task where you have no good example to show and no clear format in mind will fight you, and that resistance is information — it means the task is under-specified in your own head, and ten minutes of writing down what good actually looks like fixes it. End of week 4: three Machine-Ready tasks reliably faster, message library growing, one sticky note accumulating evidence.

## Weeks 5–8: Install the Applications

These four weeks are Chapters 4 through 7, one per week, in the order that matches most owners' pain. If your audit disagrees with this order — if money-chasing hurts you more than enquiries — run yours instead; the sequence below is the default, not a rule.

**Week 5 — the message engine.** Enquiry replies and review responses fully in workflow, on every real instance. This is the highest-frequency task, so the hours show up first and the confidence builds fastest. Track volume: replies sent, average time from enquiry to response, and your own send-with-minor-edits rate.

**Week 6 — marketing that ships.** The twenty-minute harvest, the assembly-line batch of three formats per item, scheduled in the same sitting. Two to four pieces live by Sunday, plus one real email to your list (create it if it does not exist — a one-line signup promise, nothing more). The metric that matters is not reach; it is that content went out every week without a heroic Tuesday.

**Week 7 — quotes and follow-up.** Your quote structure written down once, one live quote drafted through it, and the three-touch follow-up sequence built and calendared. Count the quotes sitting unsent from the last month — that is the pile being cleared. From this week, nothing sits: enquiry to quote within a day, follow-up one, four-or-five, and fourteen days later, every time.

**Week 8 — the back office.** Every overdue invoice chased with a drafted nudge, the twice-a-year document you have been avoiding completed by dictation and structure, and the five-minute Friday note habit started. Baseline check: pull the sticky note and total the hours reclaimed across all eight weeks. Most owners at this point are holding eight to twelve hours a week, with quotes moving and the inbox lighter — and, importantly, no automations built yet. This is still all you, typing into a fast tool.

## Weeks 9–11: Install the Automations

**Week 9 — the first flow.** Chapter 8 end to end: the enquiry trigger, the gathered details, your week-5 prompt, the DRAFT-prefixed approval email to yourself. Ten test situations, count the approval rate, let it run untouched for the remaining days. The target metric: drafts you would send with at most one word changed, measured honestly, because overestimating this is how an unedited draft reaches a customer.

**Week 10 — the second and third flows.** Booking-to-confirmation-and-log, and the Monday-morning week summary with follow-up drafts attached. Both approval-gated, both boring, both running untouched from week 10 onward — if a flow needs weekly tinkering, its spec is wrong, and the fix is the specification, not more tinkering.

**Week 11 — the tool audit.** Sit down with your actual software subscriptions — email, accounting, booking, CRM, website, social scheduler, storage — and for each one ask two questions: does this have AI features I am not using, and is there anything here I am paying for twice at a different tier? This unglamorous hour routinely finds more recoverable time and money than any new purchase, because the features arrived in updates you did not read. Cancel one redundant subscription; adopt one dormant feature. That is the week's whole assignment and it is worth more than it looks.

At the end of week 11 the machine is running: drafts prepared overnight, summaries assembled before you wake, the queue of ready work replacing the queue of nothing-started. Check the hours against the original sticky note. Ten was the target; twelve to fifteen is the common landing.

## Week 12: Review, Decide, and Choose the Next Thing

Two hours, alone, with the numbers: hours reclaimed per week versus the week-1 baseline; quotes sent and their speed; invoices chased; content shipped; approval rates on the drafts; anything that went wrong and what the fix was. Then the decision the whole plan was building toward — not "is AI good" but "what does the business do with these hours?"

There are only three honest answers and each is a real strategy. **Grow**: spend the reclaimed hours on sales conversations, the second location, the product line — the thing you said you had no time for, now with the time itemized. **Deepen**: raise quality — better proposals, faster response, more careful work, higher prices justified by visible professionalism. Or **take the time**: Fridays, mornings, the school pickup — a business that runs in fewer hours is not a smaller business, and for plenty of owners it is the entire point. Any of these is a win; what is not acceptable is the hours quietly dissolving back into the unsorted week, which is the default outcome if you skip this review entirely.

Then choose the next quarter's focus: the fourth automation, the next application in a new territory (hiring documents, supplier negotiations, a second channel), or a deliberate pause to let what you have settle. The plan's last instruction is also its most important: stop adding things for a month. Systems that run untouched for four weeks become part of how the business works; systems that are still being edited at week twenty never do.

## If It Is Not Working

Three honest patterns, and what each one actually means.

**"The drafts are bad."** Almost always the inputs, almost never the model. Missing facts (the model cannot know your prices — supply them), missing format (ask for length and structure, not "professional"), missing example (show it one message you liked). If all three are present and output is still poor, the task may be one of judgment rather than pattern — some work genuinely is not patterned, and the audit's YOU pile was trying to tell you that. Cross it off; not everything is automatable and the plan survives one crossed-off item.

**"I keep forgetting to use it."** A workflow problem, not a discipline problem. Tie each tool to a trigger you already have — enquiry arrives, open the tool; Friday, run the back-office batch — rather than intending to "use AI more." The two sticky notes are the other half: what gets measured weekly gets done weekly, and what is invisible gets dropped within a fortnight, regardless of motivation.

**"It saved time and I do not know where it went."** The most common week-12 surprise, and the reason the plan demands baselines in week 1 and a review in week 12. Reclaimed hours do not hold themselves; something fills them, usually the same reactive busyness that filled them before, because the inbox expands to meet the capacity you gave it. The fix is structural: put one thing in the diary that could not fit before — the sales block, the early finish, the second stream — so the hours are assigned to something on purpose. A time saving that is not given a job gets given to your inbox instead, every time.

## The Honest Horizon

One page for what this book will not promise.

The tools will change — models improve, prices move, features migrate between the tiers you are paying for, and the specific instructions in the appendix will be outdated in some respects within a year while the structure around them (role, facts, format, review, boundary) will not, because that structure is just briefing a competent worker well, which has not changed in a hundred years of management and will not change next year either.

The economics will get more competitive, not less. As these tools become ordinary, the advantage they buy you shrinks toward zero — the owners who took ten hours back in year one were buying something scarce, and in year three everyone has it. Which means the durable returns are not the hours themselves but what the hours are spent on: the relationships, the reputation, the judgment, the one thing you build that is not patterned and therefore not available to anyone with a subscription. The tools are a means to more of that time, not a substitute for it.

And the goal was never a business that runs without you. It is a week where the patterned work is handled by patterned tools, so that the irreplaceable work — the judgment, the conversations, the taste that customers are actually paying for — happens in hours that still have some margin left in them. That is achievable in ninety days, with what you have just read, by an owner of an ordinary small business who does one thing per week and checks the numbers.

Start Monday. Do the audit.
